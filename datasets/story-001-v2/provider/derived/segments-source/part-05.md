The hand came free from the terminal block with a wet, tacky sound, skin peeling from oily steel in strips that stung with each movement. The conductor slipped from my fingers, copper bright against the dark grating, and I left it there. My palm throbbed, a steady beat that matched the silence where the belt had roared. Burnt copper hung in the air, sharp against the wool scent that still clung to my jacket from the city-zone, and beneath it, the sour tang of scorched insulation.

I pushed myself up. My knees struck the grating first, then my boots found purchase, slick under the soles. I turned away from the sorting line. The companion was gone, carried into the chute by metal that no longer moved. The corridor stretched ahead, empty, the hatch waiting at the far end where we had entered together. I walked toward it, each step loud against the grating, the burn in my left hand tightening with every flex of my fingers.

The hatch yielded when I pushed. Cold air rushed in from the boundary gap, smelling of rusted pipes and damp concrete. I stepped through, the green light above the door still blinking, steady now, indifferent. The door swung shut behind me with a sound like metal settling into its frame, final and quiet. I kept moving, boots clicking on the corridor floor, back toward the City-Zone edge where the brownout had first caught us. My burned hand hung at my side, fingers curled, the skin raw where the current had passed. The smell followed me, burnt copper and wet wool, a residue that would not wash off.

At the boundary, I paused. The gap between zones lay open, narrow and unmarked, and I crossed it without looking back. The city-zone lights flickered overhead, dim from the brownout, casting long shadows across the street. I walked until the factory corridor was nothing but a memory behind me, until the grating gave way to pavement and the cold air no longer carried the scent of scorched wire.

My left hand throbbed. I held it against my chest, the burn a constant presence, the skin tight and sensitive where the terminal had seized me. The conductor remained in the factory, copper cooling on the grating where I had dropped it. I did not go back.

In the quiet of my room, I listened for the whistle. It should have come with the line stop, a high thin tone cutting through the silence, the signal that the trick had worked. I remembered it from childhood, a frequency that had accompanied the wire touch, clear and distinct. But the silence held no such sound. Only the absence remained, a gap where a whistle should have been, leaving me uncertain whether I had ever heard it at all.
