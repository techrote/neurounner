The smell hit me first, thick and wrong, like burnt copper and wet wool. It wasn't the clean ozone of a standard fault; this was the scent of something organic being ground into the metal. The hum started as a vibration in the floorboards, a low thrum that traveled up through my soles and settled in my teeth. I stood before the sorting gantry, my fingers tracing the cold, oily rail where the conveyor belt's teeth should have been. It wasn't just moving; it was grinding. The machinery had a rhythm, a desperate, stuttering cadence that spoke of a loop running too long, too hot. I watched the red lights on the sensor arms flicker, their lenses scanning the incoming stream of scrap metal and auto-parts. The line remembers wrongly. That was the phrase that kept looping in my head, a glitch in the system. A heavy, rusted casing slid onto the belt, its surface pitted and dark. The sensor arm swiveled, a mechanical eye blinking red, then green, then red again. It was a cycle of error, a repeated mistake that the machine had learned to ignore. The gears were turning with a sound like grinding stones, the metal screeching against the belt. My companion was waiting near the intake chute, their hands hovering over the emergency override, but they were too far away to reach the critical junction. It saw the shape, not the function. It saw the metal, not the danger. The conveyor belt moved forward, relentless and indifferent, carrying the misclassified core toward the heart of the furnace. I had to act. I stepped forward, my boots clicking on the grating, the smell of ozone and burnt oil filling the air. The gears were turning faster now, the vibration intensifying. I had to break the loop, to stop the hum, before the line's memory of the wrong path became permanent. I reached for the manual cut-off, my hand slipping on the slick grating as the belt roared closer, the heat radiating against my skin, the line stopping just inches from my grasp, the companion still standing on the wrong side of the moving metal.
