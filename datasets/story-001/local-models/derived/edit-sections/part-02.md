Dust. Fine, gray grit that tastes like burnt copper on the tongue. I push the heavy iron door open, the hinges screaming a dry, metallic shriek that cuts through the thick, stagnant air. The brownout has turned the city into a suffocating lung, breathing in the heat and spitting out ash that coats my boots. My companion waits inside, huddled against a wall of rusted pipes, their face pale against the dim, flickering light of a dying bulb. They are shivering, not from cold, but from the sudden, violent drop in pressure that rattles the very bones of the building. I tell them to stay put, but my hand trembles as I reach for the emergency release lever, my fingers slipping on the slick, oily surface. The path isn't a straight line. The walls here are high, made of cold stone that feels like it's breathing, expanding and contracting with the failing grid. I push past them, my boots crunching on gravel and broken glass, the sound echoing too loudly in the sudden silence. The air is thick with dust, swirling in the weak current of the brownout, masking the faint hum of the machines that used to keep the lights on. I keep moving, my heart pounding, trying to ignore the strange whistling sound that sometimes cuts through the silence. It's not a machine sound, not really. It's the sound of something old waking up, or maybe something trying to scream, a frequency that vibrates in my teeth. I have to go the long way, the dangerous way, to avoid the trap. But the determination is a lie, a thin veneer over the panic rising in my chest. I stand there, the dust settling on my shoulders, the silence absolute and heavy. There is no noise left, no scream, no warning. I turn back toward the city, the path ahead dark and uncertain, carrying the cost of the silence.
