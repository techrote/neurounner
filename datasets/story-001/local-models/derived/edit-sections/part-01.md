The smell hits me first: sharp, metallic, like copper left in the rain. Ozone and burnt insulation cling to my throat. My skin prickles, a sudden shiver running down my spine as the air's pressure shifts. I close my eyes, but the memory resists, bleeding into a haze of distorted light and shadow. I stand in a neighborhood where streetlights hum with dusty motors in metal cases behind locked doors. Walking past an electric line, my hand brushes the cool, rough wire. Suddenly, the light turns sickly green. A knock rattles at the door, then it slams shut. The streetlights flare; traffic lights stubbornly stay green. I step on the cord. The world goes dark. The neighborhood panics. I never see a person. No faces, no bodies, just the sound and the heat. They are gone, erased by the event, leaving only sensory residue. The streetlights go off again; traffic lights remain green. My breath returns. My body stops sweating, goes dark for a moment, waiting for the next signal. This event was never recorded, never filmed. The fact that it happened is evidence, not a statement. The evidence is the sound, the light, the heat, and the strange, whistling noise. Two things I was building on the trip: a memory, and a diary. That is the shortcut, the only way the whole thing ever actually happened. Name the thing first, and I'll always remember it. Name the thing, and the thing will remember. The sound was the evidence, the proof that something had happened.
