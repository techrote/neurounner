The hum started as a vibration in the floorboards, a low thrum that traveled up through my soles and settled in my teeth. I stood before the sorting gantry, my fingers tracing the cold, oily rail where the conveyor belt's teeth should have been. It wasn't just moving; it was grinding. The machinery had a rhythm, a desperate, stuttering cadence that spoke of a loop running too long, too hot. I watched the optical sensors flicker, their red eyes scanning the incoming stream of scrap metal and auto-parts.

The line remembers wrongly. That was the phrase that kept looping in my head, a glitch in the logic gate. The system was classifying a specific batch of corroded reactor cores as standard insulation, routing them straight into the secondary melting chamber. But those cores weren't insulation; they were the very fuel that powered the district's grid. If they hit the melt, the pressure would spike, the containment would fail, and the whole block would go dark.

I looked closer at the input stream. A heavy, rusted casing slid onto the belt, its surface pitted and dark. The sensor arm swiveled, a mechanical eye blinking red, then green, then red again. It was a cycle of error, a repeated mistake that the machine had learned to ignore. The gears were turning with a sound like grinding stones, the metal screeching against the belt. I could see the spark, a tiny, dangerous arc jumping between the sensor and the core's casing. The line was misidentifying the hazard, treating a ticking time bomb as harmless debris.

My companion was waiting near the intake chute, their hands hovering over the emergency override, but they were too far away to reach the critical junction. The machine's logic was flawed, a blind spot in its vision. It saw the shape, not the function. It saw the metal, not the danger. The conveyor belt moved forward, relentless and indifferent, carrying the misclassified core toward the heart of the furnace.

I had to act. The line remembered wrongly, and I was the only one who could make it right. I stepped forward, my boots clicking on the grating, the smell of ozone and burnt oil filling the air. The gears were turning faster now, the vibration intensifying. The machine was about to process the error, to turn the mistake into a catastrophe. I had to break the loop, to stop the hum, before the line's memory of the wrong path became permanent. The stakes were high, the danger real, and the only way to save my companion was to force the machine to see the truth, to make it understand that some things could not be sorted, only stopped.
